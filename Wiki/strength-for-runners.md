# Strength Training for Runners

## Why lift during a marathon block

- **Running economy:** Heavy and explosive strength training is repeatedly linked to better running economy (less energy used at a given pace) in trained distance runners. The usual explanation is stiffer, more efficient tendons and better neuromuscular recruitment. Worth reading: systematic reviews by Blagrove et al. (2018, *Sports Medicine*) and Rønnestad & Mujika (2014, *Scand J Med Sci Sports*).
- **Muscle retention:** High mileage plus a small calorie deficit can cost muscle. Lifting sends the "keep this" signal, which is my main goal.
- **Durability:** Stronger hips, calves and trunk handle the repetitive load of long runs better late in a race, when form breaks down.

## The interference effect

Doing endurance and strength training together can blunt strength and muscle gains compared with lifting alone. It's mostly a problem with **very high running volume** and **poor scheduling**, and it's smaller for trained people who eat enough. Ways to reduce it:
- Separate runs and lifts by **6+ hours** when they fall on the same day. Run first if the run is the priority.
- Put **heavy lower-body work on hard-run days or well before key runs**, so easy days stay easy and the long run is fresh.
- Eat enough total calories and protein. Under-fueling makes interference worse.

## Scheduling around the Saturday long run

| Days before long run | What's OK for legs |
|---|---|
| 4+ (Tue) | Heavy squats, hinges |
| 2 (Thu) | Light single-leg, balance, calves |
| 1 (Fri) | Nothing loaded |
| Day after (Sun) | Upper body, core, light glute work, mobility |

## Periodizing across the block

| Block | Lifting emphasis | Sessions/wk | Reps / effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base | Build muscle and strength | 2 (full body) | 6–10 reps, 4+ reps in reserve |
| Build | Maximal strength, add power | 2 (full body) | 4–6 reps, 4+ in reserve, low volume; add jumps/swings |
| Peak mileage | Maintain | 2 | Keep the weight on the bar, cut sets |
| Taper | Stay sharp | 1 → 0 | Light, short; nothing heavy within ~5–7 days of race |

Two full-body sessions a week is enough to keep and build muscle for most people, as long as each muscle group gets trained hard twice.

## Training far from failure

Stopping 4+ reps short of failure keeps sessions short and keeps your legs fresh for running. The research tradeoff:
- **Strength:** gains are about the same whether or not you go close to failure, as long as the weight keeps creeping up.
- **Muscle size:** going closer to failure builds somewhat more muscle. Leaving reps in reserve still builds muscle, just more slowly.
- **What that means here:** 2 × 30 min at a comfortable effort will reliably *keep* muscle through the block, with modest gains. Steady progression (adding small amounts of weight) matters more than how hard any single set feels.

The core idea: **cut volume (sets) before intensity (weight)**. Strength and muscle are kept surprisingly well on low volume as long as the loads stay heavy.

## What to prioritize for runners

- **Squat and hinge patterns:** quads, glutes, hamstrings
- **Single-leg work:** running is a single-leg sport (lunges, split squats, single-leg RDL)
- **Calves and soleus:** they absorb a lot of load per stride. Train them heavy and often.
- **Trunk anti-rotation / anti-lateral flexion:** Pallof press, side plank, suitcase carry
- **Upper back:** posture late in long runs
